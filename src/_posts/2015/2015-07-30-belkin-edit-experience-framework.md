## The situation

In 2012 Belkin's brand had adjectives. By people, for people. Respects your intelligence. Respectfully irreverent. Good thoughts, but a long way from any particular decision about a screen. A reviewer trying to explain why a screen seemed wrong had the same trouble, which meant critique kept depending on taste, on gut feel.

So we set out to name the qualities we wanted people to feel and how we preferred to produce those qualities – making decisions in advance, while our thinking was clear, rather than in the middle of an argument about a product.

## What I did

We built a grid – the design directors in industrial design and UX. Down the side, four qualities: Enticing, Delightful, Interactive, Thoughtful. Across the top, the stages of somebody's life with the product – discovery, start up, setup and first use, daily use, and backstage. Then we filled in the cells.

![The left half of a whiteboard grid, with columns for Discovery and Start Up and rows for Enticing, Delightful, Interactive and Thoughtful.](/assets/images/2026/09/edit-matrix-left.jpg)
*June 2012. Left half: Discovery and Start Up.*

![The right half of the same whiteboard grid, with columns for Setup and First Use, Daily Use and Backstage.](/assets/images/2026/09/edit-matrix-right.jpg)
*Right half: Setup and First Use, Daily Use, Backstage.*

A grid like this helps us not forget anything: put something in every box, or leave it out on purpose.

Interactive at setup and first use: obvious first step, minimize decisions, minimize repetition. Thoughtful at setup and first use: smart defaults, minimize pitfalls. Delightful during daily use: add unexpected deluxe to the key moments. Enticing during daily use: present an opportunity at the right time, offer bite-sized opportunities for payoff, leave opportunities to brag.

None of those are adjectives. Each one is something a product does or fails to do.

## Small enough to keep in mind

Twenty cells is a good way to cover the ground, but hard to bring to a review. The published version gave each quality a what, a where, and a how, on one page.

![A one-page sheet titled Belkin Software Guidelines, with the four qualities as columns and What, Where and How as rows.](/assets/images/2026/09/edit-software-guidelines.jpg)
*The same thinking, restructured for use. The stages became a "where" for each quality rather than a column of their own.*

Each quality also got an icon and the sentence we wanted to hear a person say; those sentences would come up in conversation later. Enticing is "wow, I didn't know I could do that." Delightful is "ooh, that's cool." Interactive is "I want to do that again." Thoughtful is "I heart Belkin."

## A way to grade a product

A framework that only describes is easy to nod at. The scorecard made it a task: sixteen guidelines, each rated fails, begins to meet, fulfills, or exemplifies, with an executive summary at the bottom and a worksheet page per quality for notes.

![The EDIT UI Guidelines Scorecard, listing sixteen guidelines with rating circles and a four-point scale.](/assets/images/2026/09/edit-scorecard.jpg)
*You could sit down with a product and grade it, and two people grading separately could compare.*

The worksheet pages carry the whiteboard cells almost verbatim. "Obvious first step" became "make the first step obvious." "Leave opportunities to brag" became "make it social – enable bragging at the right time."

A simpler report card followed in 2014, describing product quality in a person's words rather than a designer's.

![A Belkin Product Report Card grading products A+ through F with a plain-language sentence for each grade.](/assets/images/2026/09/edit-report-card.jpg)
*A+ is "it is something I can fall in love with." F is "it is highly frustrating or broken much of the time," with a note about angry support calls. EDIT says what to do; this says how it landed.*

## What it was used on

The first thing we did with it was re-examine the products we already had, going through our apps stage by stage to find where they were weak and what was worth fixing first. That gave us a plan: benchmark the apps we admired, write the guidelines, prototype against them, relaunch. We ran Wemo through it, and applied it to later Belkin products.

## What it missed

We didn't get everything right…

"Pip blossom" was a specific logo animation – the Belkin pip logo blossoming open as the app started. Active, exuberant, and among the fastest animations we had. It sat under Delightful at start up and went into the guidelines, but it was too slow for that context. Somebody opening an app is in a hurry to get started, and at that moment even a well-made piece of delight is a delay.

And one of the sixteen guidelines didn't work as written. "Keep It Simple" was published and agreed with and never acted on, because everyone read it as "take things away" – a definition that runs out the moment the product needs the things you'd be taking. Making it usable took a second pass and a separate argument, which I've written about in [Simple is really…](/2026/09/04/simple-is-really/).
