I've long felt that the double-diamond design process was poorly explained, and my attempts to explain it to engineering-led organizations were typically met with cries of "waterfall!" Never mind that "waterfall" specifically means big design up front, with a design lock gating implementation. "Waterfall" has become an easy epithet for any process with a sequence.

Double diamond was well-intentioned. It hinged on two ideas: a reminder to generate possibilities before evaluating them (the diamonds), and a reminder to be sure what benefit you wanted to deliver (the first diamond) before figuring out a solution (the second diamond). And yes! You should do both of those.

Patrick Neeman argues that [The UX Double Diamond is dead, and in AI only one survives for software](https://uxdesign.cc/the-ux-double-diamond-is-dead-and-in-ai-only-one-survives-for-software-heres-how-to-adapt-f63b57c524ed). And he's not wrong, exactly. But it has always been dead. Projects never really worked that way, try as we might.

Real-world projects follow much more a "cafeteria model" than strict-process affairs. We pick and choose among the things a "complete" project might contain, skipping things we think we don't need or don't have time for. Yes, you should understand the customer problem before offering a solution. Sometimes that happens. Yes, you should think of multiple ways to do a thing before doing it. Sometimes that happens. Yes, you should consider the benefit your team wants to deliver before coming up with ways to deliver that benefit. Sometimes that happens. Not every project is going to follow every available step.

If you look at the good projects, however slimmed-down they are, the generate-then-evaluate pattern persists. You will see single diamonds all over the place as needed. These diamonds can be large or small depending on appetite and importance.

Neeman's single diamond is one of these. In his version, you express a desired outcome as a brief, feed it to the agent, have it make possibilities (generate), choose among them (evaluate), and ship. But he's sort of hopping over how you got to that outcome in the first place – how you found the benefit you hope to deliver and how you selected it over others. That's a diamond!

And there are more diamonds. You might use a diamond when you run into a usability problem. The customers are struggling to finish a process? What are the different things we could try to fix this? That's the generative portion. Which one can those customers accomplish the easiest? That's the evaluative part.

You might use a diamond when you run into a technical limitation. We can't reliably read this bit of data from the device at this point in the setup process. What can we do to work around this problem? Read it again later? Defer the decision as long as we can? Read it in advance? Assume the default until it's not safe to do so? Ask the user? That's the generative portion. Which one brings the customer closer to their goal safely? That's the evaluative part.

Wording problem? Little baby diamond. Don't write one fix to that label; write five, then choose.

The diamond works because of how brains work. The Executive Control Network (ECN) is responsible for focus, cognitive control, task evaluation, and screening out irrelevant stimuli. The Default Mode Network (DMN) does the messy, associative thinking, daydreaming, and memory retrieval. [Penn State psychologist Roger Beaty](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/making-connections-psychologist-explores-neuroscience-creativity) points out that the two systems need to "get along" for creativity to be effective, and that means switching back and forth between systems rather than letting them collide. The VP (ECN) who walks into the brainstorming meeting (DMN) and interrupts by complaining about all the dumb ideas ruins the exercise.

You generate better ideas when you know the domain. So those diamonds work even better when they [look more like frying pans](/2022/11/09/single-diamond-the-basic-form-of-the-creative-process/). There's a period before generation, a handle sticking out to the left, where you immerse yourself in the customer or technical situation to make your concept generation more effective. Neeman has a stubby little handle on his diamond, where he explains the desired benefit to the agent.

I wrote up one tiny AI-assisted diamond in [Quick prototypes beat thinking your way to one answer](/2026/06/08/quick-prototypes-beat-thinking/). I used the agent to quickly create thirteen options, rejected half right away, winnowed those remaining down to three pretty good ones, brought those to customers and the team, and settled on one everyone was happy with. Yes, the prototype squelched a lot of anticipated "gold plating" debate. But at bottom it was a diamond, small and fast.

A diamond is just a reminder to generate before you evaluate – that to have good ideas you need to have many, and then you need to choose among them. Have your customer help choose as well. The common advice to explain your project to the agent, work on a plan together, have it propose multiple ways forward – there's a diamond in there. There's an immersion phase (discussing the problem in depth), a generation phase (making several possibilities), and an evaluation phase (dropping the ones that turned out bad, re-working the interesting but not-quite right ones, selecting the few best ones for further discussion). And if your project is going well, that won't be the only diamond.
