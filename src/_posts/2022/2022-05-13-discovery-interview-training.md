Cayuse's leadership had told the product team it was to understand the markets we serve, understand why customers do what they do, focus ont he challenges of real users, and actually talk to users to accomplish these. But the process the team actually followed had no step where any of that happened. Product managers, buried in feature commitments, took in requests and turned them into features. When leadership did a start/stop/continue exercise at a meeting in Portland, "train on customer interviewing" landed on the start list.

So I ran a short training program for product managers, product owners, tech leads, and designers: three sessions over four weeks, with homework in between. Session one made the case for discovery and taught the one thing I think matters most – a good research question and the topic list that grows out of it. Session two covered how to schedule, structure, and run the interview itself. Between the two, everyone paired up with a buddy and wrote a research question and topic list; after the second, each pair went and did an interview, then in the third session we debriefed as a group.

I told them at the outset that I'd rather they interview a little badly than not at all. The point was to demystify the technique enough that people would start, then get better with practice. These are the two decks I presented. (The debrief session didn't require slides.)

![discovery-interview-training page 2](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-2.png)
*Page 2: The plan for the month. Three sessions, two stretches of homework with a buddy, and a debrief at the end. People came in with different levels of comfort with interviewing, and that was fine – this was meant to lay some groundwork and get in a little practice, or serve as a refresher for anyone who'd done it before.*

![discovery-interview-training page 3](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-3.png)
*Page 3: Today's topics: the aim of discovery, a core technique, a core element of that technique, and your homework.*

![discovery-interview-training page 4](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-4.png)
*Page 4: This slide was already familiar to the team. VP of Product Germaine had told the team to develop a deep understanding of the markets we serve, understand why customers do what they do, focus on the real challenges of real people, and work with actual users. Discovery is how you do those things.*

![discovery-interview-training page 5](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-5.png)
*Page 5: In 2020 NN/g surveyed in-house product teams about the relationship between discovery and project success. It's a little interesting that half of projects succeed without any discovery at all. But failure and "meh" are much more likely without it, and success is much more likely with it.*

![discovery-interview-training page 6](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-6.png)
*Page 6: Another slide the team had seen before. To fulfill our mission the way we'd said we would, we needed to understand user and organizational needs and behavior, then offer benefits that reduce the most common or painful burdens. We didn't have a process that did that.*

![discovery-interview-training page 7](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-7.png)
*Page 7: At that start/stop/continue exercise a few weeks earlier, most of what came up for the product team pointed at discovery: pilot it on a single new capability, train on interviewing, build a framework for accomplishing interviews, look at support traffic for opportunities, set a customer-contact metric.*

![discovery-interview-training page 8](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-8.png)
*Page 8: There are lots of places in a release cycle where we should be in contact with actual users – every dot on this diagram. Discovery draws mainly on the three highlighted areas: the market, outcomes data from our own products, and direct research with current and prospective users.*

![discovery-interview-training page 10](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-10.png)
*Pages 9 and 10: The three research horizons. H1 is customer contact that helps us ship. H2 is customer contact that helps us pick among possible solutions. H3 is customer contact that helps us decide what opportunities exist and are valuable. Discovery aims straight at horizon three: determine IF and WHY a product, capability, or feature should be built, and what's important about it. Put another way, find a problem, see if it's significant, and see what benefit we could offer to address it. Coming up with the solution is a separate job.*

![discovery-interview-training page 11](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-11.png)
*Page 11: The activities of discovery, overlaid on the product development cycle. New benefits come from examining the actual work being done and noticing where we can help. Improvement opportunities come from current pain – support data, forum questions, summative testing, complaints, and how well we're meeting the outcomes targets we set.*

![discovery-interview-training page 12](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-12.png)
*Page 12: Interviewing is the core technique. NN/g found it used in more than half of all discoveries, and no other technique is used even half the time. The others are valuable, but interviewing is central.*

![discovery-interview-training page 14](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-14.png)
*Page 13 and 14: It isn't a core technique because it's popular. It's popular because it works. We learn things we don't already know. We trip over opportunities that aren't otherwise visible. We get a feel for the level of need and the level of potential impact, and for which parts are hardest or broken. And the people who will actually use the thing stay at the center. Also: if it doesn't fit their mental model and the rest of their job, it won't get adopted. If they don't like it, they won't recommend it. If they use it and like it, the decision-makers come back to us for more.*

![discovery-interview-training page 16](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-16.png)
*Page 15 and 16: This core technique has a central element we have to get right: the topic list. And a good topic list starts with a good research question. A good research question is about understanding people rather than the product. It concerns something we don't yet understand, and something we need to know to move forward. And it has to be answerable with the technique we've chosen – in this case, interviews.*

![discovery-interview-training page 18](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-18.png)
*Page 17 and 18: Here are the kinds of questions people usually pose first. Which of these are good research questions? Which are good for interviews? Mostly, none of them. "Which design is best?" is an A/B test. "Is this usable?" is a usability test. "Do people like it?" is a satisfaction survey. "Will people buy it?" and "Is this feature valuable?" are product/market fit questions. "What do people want?" can become a research question if you make it about a process: what's good and bad about how they do this today?*

![discovery-interview-training page 19](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-19.png)
*Page 19: A good business goal can give rise to a good research question, though. "How can we help Type I diabetics manage their condition better?" becomes "How do Type I diabetics make decisions about their self-management, and what makes that easier or harder?" "How do we reduce support costs for our WiFi router?" becomes "How do normal folks think about setting up a router, and where does our setup support or clash with their understanding?"*

![discovery-interview-training page 20](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-20.png)
*Page 20: Two examples closer to home, including one about our own process: why does the developed product look so unsatisfyingly different from the UX specifications? Notice how much of each research question is about behavior – what people do, how they think, what's hard and easy, why they do what they do. Notice also that the research question is more specific than the business question. It's not yes/no, not predictive, not leading, and it focuses on the user.*

![discovery-interview-training page 23](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-23.png)
*Pages 21–23: Behavioral questions are about observable facts, the recent past, how often a thing happens and why – and about the dimensions along which people might differ in ways that affect their behavior. Attitudinal questions are about how the difficulties make them feel, what success looks and feels like today, and the motivations that explain the behavioral "why." We care about this because the impact of what we build is both functional and emotional. One thing we're not going to ask about: the future. People are bad at predicting their own behavior. That's why NPS is controversial, and why "if we built X, would you buy it?" doesn't produce reliable answers.*

![discovery-interview-training page 24](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-24.png)
*Page 24: Some formulas for research questions. Using one doesn't guarantee a good question, and you aren't limited to these, but they're a place to start if you're stuck.*

![discovery-interview-training page 25](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-25.png)
*Page 25: The research question breaks down into topics, and the areas of the empathy map suggest what those topics should touch. I prefer a topic list to a script. When I script specific questions I have a harder time getting a conversation going. So I write down a handful of things I want to know about, maybe script a few easy questions to open with, and steer from there.*

![discovery-interview-training page 26](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-26.png)
*Page 26: A worked example, using the engineering-and-QA question from a few slides back. A few scripted questions get us talking about the recent past and what's observable about an engineer picking up a story. Then we shift to topics. A well-managed conversation can be steered to cover all of these without turning into an interrogation. Note that the topics probe thought, behavior, feeling, and interactions at different points in the process.*

![discovery-interview-training page 27](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-27.png)
*Page 27: Homework. Find a buddy. Decide what you want to learn about. Write a research question. Figure out who to interview, internal or external. Write a topic list. Bring both to the next session.*

![discovery-interview-training page 28](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-28.png)
*Page 28: The second session, a week later – accomplishing the interview.*

![discovery-interview-training page 29](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-29.png)
*Page 29: By now everyone had a research question, a topic list, and some idea of who to talk to. Now what? You need the right people in the room, you need to schedule it, you need a structure, and you need some ways to keep the conversation going. After that we critiqued the homework.*

![discovery-interview-training page 30](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-30.png)
*Page 30: Ideally two interviewers: one to run the conversation and maintain rapport, one to take notes, make sure the topic list gets covered, ask clarifying questions, and watch the clock. Taking notes beats relying on a recording – you remember more, transcripts are poor, you won't have time to review the recording anyway, and you lose the clarifying questions the notetaker thinks of while trying to capture what they hear. Ideally one interviewee. Two works if one is primary. A group session is not an interview; you'll only go deep with the loudest voice in the room, if that.*

![discovery-interview-training page 31](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-31.png)
*Page 31: Scheduling. Ask a week ahead, offer a few times, explain the general topic, remind them there's nothing to prepare, say how long it'll take, and get them somewhere quiet. Fatigue shows up around 45 minutes, so keep it under an hour. The aim is that the person doesn't have to think much to say yes, knows what to expect, and gets it on the calendar.*

![discovery-interview-training page 32](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-32.png)
*Page 32: The structure of the interview itself: opening, discussion, closing, and a debrief afterward. Each gets its own slide.*

![discovery-interview-training page 33](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-33.png)
*Page 33: The opening takes about five minutes. Introduce yourselves briefly, ask how they are – the start of rapport, if you can be sincere about it – and restate the purpose of the interview in the same terms you used when scheduling. Explain your roles, remind them of the timeframe, and check that all of that is alright, which further establishes consent. Then ask about recording, and say how the recording will and won't be used.*

![discovery-interview-training page 34](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-34.png)
*Page 34: The discussion is the bulk of the time, 30 minutes or more. Open with the "further rapport" questions from the top of your topic list, then work the list flexibly. The notetaker makes sure things are clear and nothing gets missed outright.*

![discovery-interview-training page 35](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-35.png)
*Page 35: Closing, five to ten minutes. The notetaker calls time. Anything totally missed on the topic list? Ask one expansive question if there's room, and always ask "is there anything else you'd like to add before we finish?" Ask permission to email with follow-up questions – and promise not to abuse it. Then thank them.*

![discovery-interview-training page 36](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-36.png)
*Page 36: Debrief as soon as possible afterward. Don't skip this. Go over notes and impressions: what stood out, what you learned, what was different from other interviews, what you're still curious about. Then talk about how the interview itself went.*

![discovery-interview-training page 37](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-37.png)
*Page 37: Question styles. Closed-ended questions prompt short answers – yes/no, a number, a simple fact. "What color is your shirt?" Open-ended questions prompt some thought and a longer answer: "Why did you choose that shirt today?" An open-ended question still needs enough specificity to latch onto. All but the earliest questions in an interview should be open-ended; closed-ended ones are for getting into specifics or restarting a stalled conversation.*

![discovery-interview-training page 38](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-38.png)
*Page 38: Easy, friendly questions at the start build rapport. Topical closed-ended questions that lead naturally into open-ended ones build more. "How many proposals does your office handle in a day?" "Wow, that's a lot. How do you deal with that volume?" Put a few of these at the top of your topic list.*

![discovery-interview-training page 39](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-39.png)
*Page 39: Getting more information. Your follow-up question doesn't need to be clever or tightly connected to the subject; asking for more is enough. "Say more about that?" "If it's handy, could we see one?" Focusing on a specific recent example helps, especially when you're into behavior, process, or feelings.*

![discovery-interview-training page 40](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-40.png)
*Page 40: It's okay to change the subject – to end a tangent, move along the topic list, or return to something from earlier. You don't need to apologize, even if you cut someone off. Only the barest justification is needed. Just do it and be pleasant.*

![discovery-interview-training page 41](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-41.png)
*Page 41: Expansive questions, late in the interview, broaden thinking and connect process to feeling. What makes a day an especially good day in this role, and what does a bad one look like? Is there one stupid problem you wish would just go away?*

![discovery-interview-training page 42](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-42.png)
*Page 42: For the under-responsive interviewee, fall back to factual, descriptive questions without closing them down entirely. "Walk me through the steps when…" "The most recent time you did that, what happened first? Then?" Getting someone who seems to have no strong feelings about anything to open up means starting with things they can simply recall. Openings for further questions appear on their own.*

![discovery-interview-training page 43](/assets/images/2022/05/discovery-interview-training-page-43.png)
*Page 43: New homework. Plan and schedule an interview, do it, debrief right afterward, and make your notes readable for later. Think about what went well or poorly so we can talk about it at the debrief session.*
