Connect by Cayuse is the company's annual customer conference, held online in October 2022. This was my Day 1 session: 30 minutes on how the UX design team approached its work, what we'd added to our practice over the prior year, and a few positions I hold about design that customers might not expect from a vendor. The audience was research administrators and the people who buy and run Cayuse software, so the talk stays away from design jargon and leans on examples.

The middle of the talk is the part I stand behind most. Clever, fancy, and innovative designs are nice but can confuse users – striking out in a new direction requires careful testing. People arrive at our software with thousands of hours of practice using other software, and every new interface idea is one more thing they have to learn unless it is spectacularly intuitive. I'd rather spend the innovation on the workflows these people have to accomplish – fewer steps, more automation, fewer mistakes – and keep the interface familiar. Four years and one AI boom later, I think that remains true.

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 1](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-1.png)
*Page 1: I'm Jon Plummer, director of user experience design at Cayuse, and I'm here to chat about the role of design at Cayuse.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 2](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-2.png)
*Page 2: We'll discuss some basics about what my team does, how our practice has changed over the past year, a few ways we think about our work that might be informative, see some examples of what happens when we work this way, and have a little time for questions at the end.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 3](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-3.png)
*Page 3: There's nothing secret or proprietary about good design practice. You understand, then you design, then you understand, then you design. Ideally "design" means generating several possibilities rather than one, and "understand" sometimes means helping to choose among them. It's deceptively simple.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 4](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-4.png)
*Page 4: I think of our work in three horizons. Horizon 1 is what's closest to shipping: the feature is defined and we need to make it work well and specify its behavior carefully. Horizon 2 is what's next: we have a result we want to deliver, we make concepts that might deliver it, and we choose among them. Horizon 3 is farther out: looking for opportunities and benefits, learning how people do their work and where the friction is. We learn and we design in each of them.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 5](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-5.png)
*Page 5: The activities differ by horizon. In Horizon 3 the "understand" work dominates: interviewing, watching people do tasks, surveys, summative usability testing of the software as it stands, customer advisory board discussions, and data like support contacts, forum questions, usage metrics, and the questions that come up in sales demos.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 6](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-6.png)
*Page 6: The design outputs in Horizon 3 are study findings, service blueprints, and feature progressions – if delivering a benefit takes several steps, a feature progression explains which steps, in what order.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 7](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-7.png)
*Page 7: In Horizon 2 we evaluate concepts with users, with the customer advisory board, and with our own engineers, who can tell us which are possible with the data we have. Participatory design appears in both columns; I think of it as an "understand" activity, and I'll come back to why. The design work is generating, refining, and selecting among concepts.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 8](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-8.png)
*Page 8: In Horizon 1 we run formative usability testing – can people operate a prototype of the thing – and do detailed design and specification: exactly how it works, exactly how it looks, which controls, where. When we're chewing on a problem we sometimes have to remind ourselves which horizon we're in.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 9](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-9.png)
*Page 9: A Horizon 3 tool we introduced this year: service blueprinting. In the consumer world a service blueprint describes the experience a customer should have and the coordination the company needs in order to deliver that experience. We turned it on its head and use it as a research tool, because Cayuse products sit inside organizational processes that we're here to make run more smoothly. This one, deliberately too small to read, maps an animal procurement workflow across many institutions. It came out of more than 20 interviews, and it exposes where we should help more and what isn't quite right about what we already do.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 10](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-10.png)
*Page 10: Participatory design exercises, over Zoom. Having people draw or arrange things can be more informative than having them tell you or write a paragraph. It looks like design but it's mostly understanding: the value is in people explaining what they did and what they were trying to do. We'd never lift a participant's sketch and use it directly.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 11](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-11.png)
*Page 11: Search for "double diamond" and you get a thousand process diagrams; search for "single diamond" and you get gemstones. The common thread is that each diamond is an expansion of possibilities followed by a winnowing to a chosen direction. The place organizations most often forget to put a diamond is between choosing a problem and working on a specific solution. Even very senior people leap from a problem to a single solution and work on it to the exclusion of alternatives that might be easier, cheaper, or more effective.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 12](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-12.png)
*Page 12: So I like a single diamond here: quickly generate several rough concepts, then choose among them with help from expert witnesses – customer advisory board members, key users, subject matter experts, our engineers and product managers, other designers. Over the past year we shared mockups in a couple dozen advisory board meetings, often with only one or two concepts in hand. Bringing more than that is much more powerful, and it's where I want to extend our practice.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 13](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-13.png)
*Page 13: Unmoderated remote usability testing. If someone fails a usability test it isn't because they're not good; it's because the **thing** isn't good enough. Before the pandemic this meant bringing people into a lab with a researcher taking notes and strenuously not helping. Now people can try the thing on their own time, on video, talking through their struggles, and we get results faster with far less scheduling. This is our Horizon 1 understand activity, and we're going to do a lot more of it.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 14](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-14.png)
*Page 14: To raise quality, consistency, and speed while still making room for understanding, the parts that make up the design have to become reusable. That starts with a design system – a kit of parts designers use to express designs quickly and consistently – and continues into a pattern library, so engineers and QA get the same kit as shared code. These are snapshots of our Confluence pages, where we describe the form, behavior, and accessibility requirements of each pattern and component so we don't repeat ourselves in detailed design.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 15](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-15.png)
*Page 15: There's a maturity model for this. You start with how things look – brand guidelines, component styles – then how they behave, then how they're built, then how they're tested. A year ago we had brand guidelines and some component styles. Now the behavior and accessibility criteria for the components are defined, there are guidelines for what to use when, and components are grouped into patterns. Next: code backing every component, shared features that work the same across products, and the same benefits for the team that tests the product.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 16](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-16.png)
*Page 16: The telephone game: a message put in at one end of a chain of people is distorted by the time it reaches the other end. Teams that work in sequence hit this even when communication is otherwise fine: each step makes well-intentioned adjustments and the adjustments compound into late surprises. The fix is to deal with the seeds of the surprises early and as a group. We call it the product trio – the product manager, whoever leads design for the product, and whoever leads engineering – using what they know as a team to select the way forward together. Making a habit of it has been a pursuit of ours for nine months, and it's helping.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 17](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-17.png)
*Page 17: Now some philosophy. Three things worry me as a design leader when we're working in Horizons 1 and 2: when a design seems clever, when it seems fancy, and – this might surprise you – when it seems innovative. Innovation is commonly thought of as an unmitigated good. By innovation I just mean introducing new ideas, and new ideas are great. It's where you put them that matters.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 18](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-18.png)
*Page 18: I'd much rather innovate in the process of accomplishing a thing: remove unnecessary steps, make parts automatic, make parts mistake-proof, change the way something is communicated so the right information appears at the right time. I'd rather introduce new ideas there than in the interface itself.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 19](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-19.png)
*Page 19: People learn to use software through thousands of hours of practice using other software. If we introduce ideas that are too new in the interface, that's a new thing they have to learn. We need to minimize the number of things you have to learn to be successful with our software, and make use of the thousands of hours of practice you already have.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 20](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-20.png)
*Page 20: So we favor process innovation plus interface familiarity. Things that are familiar require less learning and less training. That's impossible to do without a lot of "understand" activity in every horizon, and it means that when we're tempted to innovate in the interface we step back, look at what we're trying to accomplish, and see whether we can use what people already know instead.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 21](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-21.png)
*Page 21: It's the commonest thing for someone – a customer, a salesperson, a product manager – to come to a designer with a very specific request. We have a semi-derogatory term for it: a sandwich order. "Just put a button here that does this thing." It's tempting to take the order because you can deliver fast and satisfy the person in the moment, and when a team is under pressure sandwich orders arrive in bunches. But time and again they don't ultimately satisfy. The idea was fine but fragile, something was lost in translation, and most often there's a deeper need the order is a symptom of. Worse, an organization busy filling sandwich orders isn't learning anything. If you don't know why you're making what you're making, you can't know until too late whether you made the right thing.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 22](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-22.png)
*Page 22: Here's a real floor plan, from Zillow. As a sandwich order it looks fine: four bedrooms, two baths, here you go. Then you look closer. To get from the living room, in green, to the main bedroom you go through the dining area, the kitchen, the laundry, a bonus room, a bathroom, and a hallway – to reach a room that shares a wall with the living room. You have to learn how a thing will be used to make it well.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 23](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-23.png)
*Page 23: Another thing that matters at Cayuse: burden. The Federal Demonstration Partnership's faculty workload survey asks federally funded principal investigators how much of their time goes to activities other than the research itself. The most recent one was 2018, following 2012 and 2005. Their view is narrower than ours – PIs only, federal funding only – but framing non-research activity as burden is interesting and potentially useful to us.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 24](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-24.png)
*Page 24: Burden is any necessary activity outside of the research itself, and burden reduction is reducing the time it takes to do those activities, assuming they're necessary. I'd rather put it the other way around – make the necessary work of supporting research easier, quicker, and less error prone – but the question is whether burden is an idea we can use.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 25](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-25.png)
*Page 25: The burden percentage keeps going the wrong direction across the three surveys, which is what we're here to help with. But measuring it their way has proven tricky: they ask people to estimate. When we ask the same questions in surveys inside our apps, few people answer (they're busy, we're interrupting them) and the numbers range from the 30s to the 80s. The real problem is asking people to estimate instead of measuring how successful they are. We'll get more from saying we raised the share of submissions accepted on the first attempt, or cut the time a disclosure takes from here to here, than by asking if someone feels the burden has gone up or down.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 26](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-26.png)
*Page 26: Here's a quick quick example of design work that should launch soon, and how these practices fit into it.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 27](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-27.png)
*Page 27: Our Inventions product already had invoices, and people could preview and download them.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 28](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-28.png)
*Page 28: But sending one meant wrestling the invoice out of the system, exporting the supporting documents, writing an email in a separate program, attaching everything, and looking up the right addressees. Manual, and burdensome, to use the term.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 29](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-29.png)
*Page 29: This little link we're adding hides a lot of power. It opens a deliberately familiar email interface – To, From, Subject, a place for the body – recognizable without training, with the invoice already attached.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 30](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-30.png)
*Page 30: The supporting documents the system already knows about are listed for you to check off. No hunting, no moving files around.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 31](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-31.png)
*Page 31: Templates let you set up the emails you send regularly in advance: the message, which roles belong in the To line, what data goes in the subject.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 32](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-32.png)
*Page 32: Choose a template and the email is basically ready to go. Crafting the message becomes looking it over and adjusting if needed, instead of starting from scratch. The research behind this: user interviews, customer advisory board meetings, and sharing designs as we went to get feedback.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 33](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-33.png)
*Page 33: What's next? We'll keep experimenting with burden through other lenses – a single usability score, customer effort score – and measure success directly: how long common activities take, how often people succeed, how many visits it takes to do a habitual task, where people hesitate. Push further down the pattern library maturity model so engineering and QA have a better time implementing our designs. And always more research, more direct contact with actual users.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 34](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-34.png)
*Page 34: To that end, we depend on people who use our products talking with us. There's a short form at cayuse.com/participate; if you've filled it out already, thank you, and if you do it a second time we'll notice and it'll be fine.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 35](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-35.png)
*Page 35: Questions. One from the audience: when did Cayuse first hire a UX team? My focus over my 14 months has been process more than design skill. The designers Cayuse had, past and present, were skilled but not as organized as we are becoming, and I've worked to integrate design, product management, and engineering and to give our decisions a stronger basis in data. I am more proud of the work we are doing now than I was 14 months earlier.*

![conference-talk-ux-philosophy page 36](/assets/images/2022/11/conference-talk-ux-philosophy-page-36.png)
*Page 36: A second question about a recently acquired product: I support the designers who work on specific products, and reaching into (their work) with my own fingers would be overreaching. But the acquisition brought overlapping capabilities that we'll want to merge into a superset, and I looked forward to applying the design system and pattern library to make something better than either product alone.*
