Most companies say they care about customers. Their products rarely show it. I build ones that do – thoughtful enough that people notice, trust, and recommend them.

I lead product design where hardware and software have to work as one experience – the device, the app, the service, and the parts with no screen at all. I've done it for insulin pumps and glucose monitors at Medtronic MiniMed, and for routers, smart-home devices, and their apps at Belkin, Linksys, and Wemo, and I'd like to keep doing it – in industrial IoT, robotics, medical devices, or anywhere else people depend on a device and its software working together.

I run teams the modern way: prototypes in code and decisions grounded in customer knowledge. Both showed up at Invoca, my most recent role. My designers moved from drawing pictures of UI to prototyping in the real codebase, and I ran concept sprints that took a cross-functional team from an open question to a concept customers had tried. Two of those concepts became shipped AI agents. The same approach now reaches hardware: a device, how it behaves, and the software around it can be prototyped together, down to how the device and the app talk to each other and act as one, before anyone commits to tooling. I've been doing it in my [side projects](/sides/).

## How I think about design

A device, its app, and its service are one experience to the person using them, even when three teams build them on three schedules. I wrote about this in [Toward Coordinated Experience](/2025/07/27/toward-coordinated-experience/). AI raises the stakes: as agents and models move into products, people trust what they can inspect and correct, so the interfaces around them have to be simpler, coordinated, and usable without training. The hardest part of design is picking the right direction and keeping it intact all the way to what ships. Prototypes make the first part fast, and customers make it right: put working options in front of the people who'll use them, and they help choose the way forward.

That focus on customers isn't theoretical. I trained as a social worker and an anthropologist before I became a designer, and the habit never left: understand people before you propose anything for them.

## Where I've done it

At Medtronic MiniMed I designed across insulin pumps, continuous glucose monitors, and CareLink Pro, the software clinicians used to review their patients' pump and CGM data. The devices each held part of a patient's therapy, and CareLink Pro put those parts together into one story for the clinician. My team ran design-phase usability testing on the pump and CGM interfaces, insertion devices, and CGM remote device behavior. Early on, we asked Regulatory Affairs what to watch out for: usability and intelligibility problems common to these kinds of devices, and ones that had caused trouble before. We anticipated those problems in our design and formative testing.

Then came eleven years at Belkin, where I rose from Senior Designer to Senior Director and grew a small UX team in size and influence until it was the centralized department behind Belkin, Linksys, and [Wemo](/portfolio/wemo-smart-home-accessories-and-app/). We designed the devices' lights, sounds, and buttons together with the apps, web interfaces, and cloud services that set them up and ran them. Our router setup redesign, from the box and the printed guide to the software, cut router support calls about 70%. I'm a named inventor on [18 patent families](https://patents.google.com/?inventor=Jon+Robertsen+Plummer,Jon+Plummer,Jon+R+Plummer&assignee=Medtronic+Minimed%2c+Inc,Medtronic+MiniMed%2c+Inc.%2c+Northridge,Belkin+International%2c+Inc,Belkin+Corporation,Belkin+Components&num=100&sort=old), our products won a handful of IDSA and CES awards, and I came away with a lot of hard-won lessons about how design earns trust in engineering- and product-led organizations.

Most recently I was Director of User Experience at Invoca, where I led a design team through an AI product transformation: SMS and voice virtual agents, plus the [Call Review Console](/portfolio/call-review-console/) and AI Scorecard, which cut Customer Success Manager caseload by a fifth and helped expand Contact Centers as a customer segment. Between Belkin and Invoca I led design at Concentric Sky and Cayuse.

## How I lead

I develop designers who can hold a point of view and defend it, not just execute one. I get there by asking them the questions they should be asking, helping them find their own way to an answer. But I'm not hands-off: when the situation calls for a direct read, I give one. I interpret, I instruct, I push back. The goal is people who can think well under pressure and make good calls without being managed through every decision.
